If you own a reflector telescope, you will eventually need to collimate it. The word sounds technical, but the process is simple: you're just making sure the mirrors are lined up correctly so the light they collect reaches your eye in focus.

A misaligned telescope gives blurry, washed-out views. A well-collimated telescope shows you sharp, bright, satisfying images. It's worth learning.

## Do you actually need to collimate?

First, check whether you even need to. Stars are the best collimation test. Find a moderately bright star, defocus it slightly (turn the focuser until it goes blurry), and look at the out-of-focus disc of light.

**Correctly collimated:** The disc looks like a uniform circle with concentric rings.
**Needs collimation:** The disc is lopsided, with rings more bunched on one side.

If yours looks fine, you don't need to do anything. Most new telescopes arrive needing at least minor collimation due to shipping vibration.

## What you'll need

**Nothing essential** — you can collimate with just your eye looking down the focuser tube.

**Optional tools that make it easier:**
- A **collimation cap** (~$10): a simple cap with a central hole that helps you centre your eye perfectly. Hugely helpful for beginners.
- A **Cheshire eyepiece** (~$25): more precise, allows adjustment in one step.
- A **laser collimator** (~$30–50): fastest method but needs its own calibration.

Start without any tool — just your eye. Add tools later if you want.

## Step 1: Centre the secondary mirror (side-to-side)

Look down the focuser tube with the cap removed (or use your collimation cap). You're looking at the secondary mirror — the small mirror held in the centre of the tube by a spider vane.

You should see:
1. The secondary mirror
2. The reflection of the primary mirror in the secondary
3. The reflection of the secondary in the primary
4. The reflection of your eye (or collimation cap hole) at the centre

**Adjustment:** The secondary mirror has a central screw (usually a Phillips head) that moves it closer or farther from the focuser, and three screws around it that tilt it. Start by centering the reflection of the primary mirror within the secondary mirror. Loosen the central screw slightly and tilt the secondary using the three surrounding screws until the primary's reflection sits centred in the secondary.

Take your time. Small adjustments make a big difference.

## Step 2: Centre the primary mirror reflection

Now you're centering the reflection of the secondary (and your eye) within the primary mirror's reflection.

The primary mirror has three adjustment screws (usually thumbscrews or hex bolts) at the back of the tube. There are often locking screws next to them — loosen these before adjusting.

Adjust the primary's screws until the small reflected circle of the secondary appears dead centre in the primary's reflection. When it looks like a perfect bullseye — concentric circles all centred on each other — you're done.

## Step 3: The star test

Take your telescope outside, let it thermally equilibrate for 20 minutes (this matters — a warm tube in cold air gives distorted views regardless of collimation), and point it at a moderately bright star.

At moderate magnification with the star slightly defocused:
- Concentric rings, symmetric: **Perfect collimation**
- Rings bunched to one side: **Go back and adjust**

It usually takes 2–3 iterations between indoor setup and outdoor star testing to get it right. After a few times, you'll nail it on the first try.

## How often do you need to do this?

For a Dobsonian used regularly: check it every few sessions, but you'll often find it's fine. A solid bump or car trip will knock it out. Otherwise, collimation tends to hold well.

Some people check before every session. Others check monthly. As you get familiar with what sharp stars look like through your scope, you'll know instinctively when something's off.

## Troubleshooting

**"The secondary looks off-centre in the focuser"** — adjust the secondary's position first (central screw) before tilting it.

**"I can't see my eye reflection"** — use a collimation cap or just shine a dim red light into the focuser from above to illuminate the mirrors.

**"Stars never look sharp even after collimation"** — give the telescope 30 minutes to cool down outside. Thermal currents inside a warm tube destroy image quality regardless of collimation.

**"I made it worse"** — no problem. Start from scratch. The worst case is you spend another 10 minutes and arrive back at where you started.

## The tools worth buying

If you collimate regularly, a **Cheshire eyepiece** is genuinely worth the $25. It illuminates the mirrors and makes the alignment process much clearer. A laser collimator is faster but requires its own calibration step, which adds complexity for beginners.

For most people: collimation cap first, Cheshire eyepiece when you want to level up.
